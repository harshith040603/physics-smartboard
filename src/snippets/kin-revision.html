Distance ≥ |Displacement| — always. Equal only for straight-line motion with no turn-back.
7:30 AM. The team bus heads to the inter-school meet: 90 km east in 2 hours — then a diversion forces it 30 km back west in 1 more hour. Coach Rao: “Average speed and average velocity. Work them out before I reveal each step.”
The journey: 90 km east (2 h) → 30 km west (1 h)
Step 1 — the two base quantities.
Total distance = 90 + 30 = 120 km · Displacement = 90 − 30 = 60 km east · Time = 3 h
Step 2 — speed comes from distance.
Average speed = 120 / 3 = 40 km/h
Step 3 — velocity comes from displacement.
Average velocity = 60 / 3 = 20 km/h east — half the speed, because the bus backtracked.
Quick check: can |average velocity| ever be bigger than average speed?
Never — displacement can’t beat distance, so speed ≥ |velocity|, always. They tie only when the motion never reverses.
Stuck near the stadium, the driver brags: “First half of the distance at 30 m/s, second half at 60 — so I averaged 45!” Arjun smiles. Should he trust the driver’s maths?
Equal distances at 30 m/s and 60 m/s. The true average speed is…
Equal distances → harmonic mean: 2(30)(60)/(30+60) = 40 m/s. The bus spends twice as long on the slow leg, so the slow speed drags the average well below 45.
Rematch: the return trip runs half the TIME at 25 km/h and half at 35 km/h. Now the average speed is…
Equal times → the simple average is correct: (25+35)/2 = 30 km/h. (29.2 is the harmonic mean — that’s the equal-distances answer. The condition decides!)
Equal distances → 2v₁v₂/(v₁+v₂)  ·  Equal times → (v₁+v₂)/2. THE CONDITION decides the formula.
From his window seat Arjun stares at the speedometer needle. It reads a speed right now — no time interval at all. How is that possible? Coach Rao leans over: “Shrink the interval and see.” The bus position follows x = 2t² + 3t.
AVERAGE v over Δt (secant slope)—
INSTANTANEOUS v = dx/dt = 4t₀+3—

v = dx/dt — the average velocity over an interval shrunk to a single instant. Instantaneous speed = |v|, always.
At the stadium. Warm-up shuttle drill: out, turn, back, turn again — Arjun’s position follows x = t³ − 9t² + 24t. Twice during the drill he is at rest for a single instant… but he never stops. Press play and catch both moments.
v = 3t² − 18t + 2424.00 m/s

v = 3(t−2)(t−4) → v = 0 at t = 2 s and t = 4 s — the turning points.

Coach Rao: “Is this drill uniform motion?”
Non-uniform: v is different at every instant (a ≠ 0), so average ≠ instantaneous velocity here. Uniform motion means constant velocity — like cruise control, not a shuttle drill. And “momentarily at rest” ≠ stopped: v = 0 for one instant means he’s turning around.
The 200 m final. Out of the blocks, braking into the bend, surging down the straight, jogging back after the finish. For each moment, read v and a — speeding up or slowing down? Fastest hands in the class win.

Race analysed! Score:
SAME signs of v and a → speeding up · OPPOSITE signs → slowing down.
Negative acceleration ≠ slowing down — it just points in −x. And fast ≠ accelerating: 200 km/h at constant speed has a = 0.
Silver medal! On the ride back Coach Rao opens his clipboard. “Three equations run every constant-acceleration problem in existence. But first — when are you even ALLOWED to use them?”
CONDITION: a must be constant. That single line is the whole licence.
1 · A ball in free fall (ignore air). Equations allowed?
Allowed — a = g, constant. Free fall is the most famous constant acceleration of all (that’s the next lecture!).
2 · A particle with a = 3t. Equations allowed?
Not allowed — a changes with time. You must integrate from the definitions instead.
3 · A train braking uniformly. Equations allowed?
Allowed — “uniformly” is the magic word: constant (negative) acceleration.
4 · A mass on a spring, a = −kx. Equations allowed?
Not allowed — a depends on position. This is SHM territory, coming later in the year.
5 · Constant force on a constant mass. Equations allowed?
Allowed — constant F on constant m gives constant a (Newton’s second law, next chapter).
“Now the template,” says Coach. “List u, v, a, s, t. Mark given and asked. Then pick the equation that’s missing the variable you neither know nor want.”
Given u, a, t — asked v. Which equation?
No s anywhere in the problem → use the equation with no s: v = u + at.
Given u, v, s — asked a. Which equation?
No t → use the equation with no t: v² = u² + 2as. (This is the braking-train equation.)
Given u, a, t — asked s. Which equation?
No v → use the equation with no v: s = ut + ½at².

Coach’s trap: “From rest at 4 m/s² — how far did you go in the 5th second?”
“In the 5th second” = the one-second slice between t = 4 and t = 5: s₅ₜₕ = 0 + (4/2)(2×5−1) = 18 m. “In 5 seconds” (the whole journey) would be ½(4)(25) = 50 m — a correct answer to a different question. Underline the wording. And note the units: metres, not m/s!
